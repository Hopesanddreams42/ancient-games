Ludus Latrunculorum

the Roman game of little soldiers
⚔
The aim: capture your opponent's soldiers — by trapping them between two of yours — until they have too few pieces left, or no legal move at all.

A Brief History

Ludus latrunculorum — "the game of little soldiers," or more bluntly, "the game of brigands" — was Rome's serious strategy game, played across the empire for over four centuries, from the frontier forts of Roman Britain to the sands of Egypt. Roman writers took it seriously: the poet Ovid recommended it as a mark of a cultured mind, and the satirist Martial praised players skilled enough to win with nothing but a handful of glass counters. No rulebook survives, and the boards that have been unearthed vary in size from find to find, so — as with the other games here — this is a modern scholarly reconstruction, following the version most historians and clubs now treat as standard: an 8×8 board, twelve men each side, and the same "custodial" capture later inherited by the Viking tafl games.

How to Play

Ludus Latrunculorum, reconstructed from scattered Latin references and archaeological finds.
  1. The board and setup. An 8×8 grid. Each side has 12 identical soldiers: a full back row of 8, plus 4 more in the centre of the second row.
  2. Moving. Every soldier moves like a rook in chess: any number of empty squares in a straight line, horizontally or vertically. No diagonal moves, and pieces can't jump over each other.
  3. Capturing. Finish your move so that an enemy soldier is sandwiched directly between the piece you just moved and another of your soldiers, in a straight line — and it's captured. Moving your own piece into a gap between two enemies is always safe; only the move that closes the trap captures.
  4. Multiple captures. One move can spring several traps at once, in different directions, if it completes more than one sandwich simultaneously.
  5. Winning. You win by reducing your opponent to two soldiers or fewer (too few to trap anything with), or by leaving them with no legal move at all.
  6. A drawn game. If 60 moves pass in a row with no capture by either side, the game ends in a draw.
  7. A note on the rules. No Roman rulebook survives. Some reconstructions add a special "Dux" piece, or treat the board's edge as an automatic second trap — this version doesn't use either, following the simpler, more widely used modern standard.

Tips

Ludus Latrunculorum is a game of lines and traps — with every piece sliding like a rook, a single unguarded lane can be crossed in one move, so control of open space matters as much as the pieces themselves.
  1. Walking into a gap is always safe. You can occupy a square between two enemy pieces without being captured — only the piece that closes the trap is ever taken. Use this to plant pieces boldly in contested territory.
  2. Look for double traps. If a single move closes two sandwiches at once, you capture both. It's worth scanning for even when the more obvious capture looks smaller.
  3. Don't open long lanes carelessly. Every piece moves any distance in a straight line, so a cleared row or column can let your opponent reach further than you'd expect. Keep some friction on the board rather than clearing paths for them.
  4. Material matters more as the game thins out. You only need to reduce your opponent to two pieces, so trades that look roughly even early on become far more decisive as both sides run low. Count pieces, not just position, in the endgame.
  5. Don't let the clock run out on your advantage. If 60 moves pass with no capture, the game's a draw — so if you're ahead, look to force exchanges rather than just holding your ground.
