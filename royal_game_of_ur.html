A Brief History

The Royal Game of Ur is one of the oldest board games ever found, dating to around 2600–2400 BCE in the Sumerian city of Ur, in what is now southern Iraq. The game went on to spread across the ancient Near East and remained popular for roughly 3,000 years. British archaeologist Leonard Woolley excavated several boards from royal tombs there in the 1920s, but for decades no one knew how it was actually played. The rules remained a mystery until the 1980s, when Irving Finkel of the British Museum translated a Babylonian clay tablet — itself written around 177 BCE, some 2,000 years after the game was buried — that described exactly how to play.

How to Play

The Royal Game of Ur, reconstructed from a 2,000-year-old cuneiform tablet.
  1. The board. Two 3×4 blocks are joined by a narrow bridge — 20 squares in total. Your own two rows at each end are private to you; the middle row is a shared "war path" where both sides can meet.
  2. Your path. A piece's route is shaped like a stretched-out "S": it enters on your own private row and travels 4 squares to the end of that block, then drops into the shared middle row and crosses all 8 squares of it — from the same end where your private row started, straight across to the far side. From there it climbs back into your own private row, but now at the opposite end of the board, and finishes the last 2 squares before leaving the board entirely. In short: your lane, then the shared middle, then the far end of your own lane again.
  3. The pieces. Each player has 7 pieces that start off the board, race along their own path, and must reach the far end and go "off" to score.
  4. Rolling. Each turn you roll four ancient pyramid-shaped dice. Each one lands marked or blank, and your move total is simply how many landed marked — from 0 to 4.
  5. Moving. Use your roll to move one piece that many squares along your path, or to bring a brand-new piece onto the board. You may not land on a square already held by one of your own pieces.
  6. Capturing. Land exactly on an opponent's piece while it sits in the shared middle row, and it's captured — sent all the way back to start. Nothing in your own private rows can ever be captured.
  7. Rosettes. The five flower-marked squares are safe — a piece there can never be captured — and landing on one earns you an extra roll immediately.
  8. Bearing off. To take a piece off the board at the far end, you must roll the exact number of squares remaining. Rolling too high means that piece simply can't move.
  9. Winning. The first player to get all 7 pieces off the board wins. If a roll leaves you with no legal move at all, your turn simply passes.
