A Brief History

Senet is the oldest board game ever discovered, with painted boards appearing in Egyptian tomb art as far back as around 3100 BCE — making it older than the pyramids. It was immensely popular for millennia, evolving over time from a simple race game into something laden with religious meaning: by the New Kingdom, senet had come to represent a symbolic journey through the afterlife, and boards were placed in tombs, including Tutankhamun's, to help the deceased navigate the underworld. No complete rulebook has ever been found, so every modern version, including this one, is a scholarly reconstruction, chiefly drawing on the research of Egyptologist Timothy Kendall and the earlier work of Gustave Jéquier.

How to Play

Senet, reconstructed from tomb paintings and centuries of scattered evidence.
  1. The board. 30 squares in three rows of ten, travelled in a snaking path: row one left to right (1–10), row two right to left (11–20), row three left to right again (21–30), then off the board.
  2. Setup. Each player has 5 pieces, already on the board, alternating along the first row: yours on the odd or even squares from 1–10.
  3. Throwing. Each turn you throw four flat sticks. Your move is simply how many land marked-side up — and throwing a 1, 4, or 6 (all four sticks blank) earns you an extra throw straight away.
  4. Capturing. Land exactly on a single opposing piece and you swap places with it — your piece takes that square, theirs goes back to where you started from. Two or more of your pieces in an unbroken row can't be swap-captured at all.
  5. Blockades. Three or more of one side's pieces in an unbroken row can't be jumped over by the other side at all — not landed on, not passed. A row of protected or blocking pieces has to stay within a single row of the board: squares 10 and 11, and squares 20 and 21, sit next to each other where the path turns a corner, but they don't count as neighbours for this rule.
  6. The House of Water (square 27). Land here and your piece is washed all the way back to square 15 — the House of Rebirth, which is itself a safe square: no piece can be swap-captured there.
  7. The final houses (26, 28, 29, 30). These are safe too — no piece can be swap-captured there. Square 26 is a mandatory stop: you can't move a piece straight past it into the final stretch, only onto it. Squares 28, 29, and 30 then hold a piece until you throw the exact number shown to finally bear it off — 3, 2, and 1 respectively.
  8. Winning. The first player to bear all 5 pieces off the board wins. If a throw leaves you with no legal move, your turn simply passes.
