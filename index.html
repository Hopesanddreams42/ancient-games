Board Games From History

Long before chess, before Monopoly, before your phone — people crowded round a board, threw dice carved from bone or corn, and tried to outwit each other. These games are rebuilt from tomb paintings, royal burials, sagas, and stone floors worn smooth by ancient hands. Play them right now, against a friend or a very determined opponent.

Ancient Egypt · c. 3100 BCE

Senet

The oldest board game ever found — older than the pyramids. Egyptians played it for fun, then for their souls: by the New Kingdom it had become a symbolic journey through the afterlife, and a board was buried with Tutankhamun to help him find his way. Its race down a track of squares is the direct ancestor of nearly every roll-and-move game since.

Sumer, Mesopotamia · c. 2600 BCE

The Royal Game of Ur

Buried in royal tombs and dug up in the 1920s, this game sat unsolved for sixty years — nobody knew the rules until Irving Finkel of the British Museum translated a 2,000-year-old Babylonian tablet in 1980, finally spelling them out. Rosette squares, safe lanes, pyramid dice: play it and you're using rules a Babylonian scribe wrote down for an already-ancient game.

Rome · by the 1st century BCE

Ludus Latrunculorum

"The game of little soldiers" — or more bluntly, brigands. Ovid recommended it as proof of a cultured mind; Martial admired players skilled enough to win with a handful of glass counters. Legionaries carried it the length of the empire, and its capture-by-surrounding rule is the direct ancestor of the Viking tafl games.

Widely diffused · attested by the 1st century CE

Nine Men's Morris

Scratched into Bronze Age stone in Ireland, into the ruins of Troy, and carved into the steps of a Sri Lankan monastery under King Mahadithika Maha-Naga. Ovid describes it in the Ars Amatoria. Unusually for a game this old, it was never forgotten — played under one name or another, more or less continuously, right up to today's pub tables.

Viking Scandinavia · 5th century CE onward

Hnefatafl

"The king's table" — a siege game where one side defends a king trying to break out, and the other tries to trap him. Icelandic sagas describe gods and kings playing it. No complete rulebook survived, so this version is stitched together from archaeology and from a related Sámi game, tablut, whose rules the naturalist Carl Linnaeus recorded in 1732.

West Africa · evidenced by the 6th–7th century CE

Oware

A sowing-and-capturing game from the Akan peoples of Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire, part of the vast mancala family played across Africa and Asia. Unlike some games here, Oware's rules were never lost — it's been played continuously for centuries and is still fiercely competitive today, with strong players calculating ten moves ahead, chess-grandmaster style.

Maya lowlands · undated, still played today

Puluc

A "running fight" where captured soldiers aren't removed but taken prisoner — marched along by their captor until they're rescued or killed. Spanish chroniclers barely mention it; almost everything we know comes from Mopan and Kekchi Maya communities in Belize and Guatemala who still play it now. This version follows R.C. Bell's 1960 reconstruction.
