Nine Men's Morris

an ancient game of mills, carved in stone across three continents
◈
The aim: line up three of your own men — a "mill" — to capture your opponent's pieces, until they're reduced to two men or have no legal move left.

A Brief History

Nine Men's Morris is one of the most widely travelled games of the ancient world. Boards have been found scratched into Bronze Age stone in Ireland, into the ruins of Troy, and carved into the steps of the hilltop monastery at Mihintale in Sri Lanka — datable to the reign of King Mahadithika Maha-Naga, around the 1st century CE. You'll often see it claimed the game goes back even further, to a carving in an Egyptian temple at Kurna from around 1400 BCE — but that particular carving can't actually be dated with any confidence, so it's included here as a famous claim rather than a fact. The Romans certainly knew it well: Ovid describes it directly in the Ars Amatoria, and boards have turned up scratched into pavements and shop counters across the empire, as far from Rome as the legionary camps of Roman Britain. Unlike most games this old, its rules were never lost — it has been played, under one name or another, more or less continuously ever since.

How to Play

Nine Men's Morris, played much as it has been for roughly two thousand years.
  1. The board. Twenty-four points, arranged as three nested squares joined by lines at their midpoints. Pieces sit on the points and move along the drawn lines only.
  2. Placing. Each side has nine men. Taking turns, you place one man at a time on any empty point until all eighteen are on the board.
  3. Forming a mill. Line up three of your own men along any drawn line — straight, not diagonal — and you form a "mill." The instant you do, remove one of your opponent's pieces from the board.
  4. Protected pieces. You can't remove a piece that's currently part of one of your opponent's own mills — unless every single one of their pieces is in a mill, in which case any piece is fair game.
  5. Moving. Once all eighteen men are placed, turns become moves: slide one of your own men along a line to an empty neighbouring point. Forming a new mill this way — even re-forming one you just broke — captures exactly as before.
  6. Flying. If you're ever reduced to exactly three men, they gain the power to leap to any empty point on the board, not just an adjacent one.
  7. Winning. Reduce your opponent to two men, or leave them with no legal move on their turn, and you win.

Tips

Nine Men's Morris is a game of position, not material rushes — with perfect play it's a known draw, so the real skill is making your opponent's next move harder than yours.
  1. Fight for the crossroads. Points where four lines meet are worth far more than the corners, where only two lines cross — they give you more ways to move and more mills to threaten. Don't spend your early placements on corners just because they feel safe.
  2. Watch for the swinging mill. Two mills sharing a single point let you open one and close the other on alternate turns, capturing a piece every time. It's the strongest structure in the game — build towards one when you can, and block your opponent's the moment you spot it forming.
  3. Threaten two mills at once during placement. A point that completes two different potential mills forces your opponent to choose which one to block, and they can only stop one.
  4. Don't strand your own pieces. Once placement ends, you can only move to an adjacent open point — a piece boxed in on all sides is dead weight until you're down to three and can fly again. Keep an escape route open.
  5. Play for complications when things are equal. Since the game is a draw with correct play on both sides, sitting back rarely wins it for you — look for the move that gives your opponent the most ways to go wrong, not just the safest one for you.
