Oware

a West African sowing game
🌰
The aim: capture more seeds than your opponent by sowing carefully and timing your captures.

A Brief History

Oware belongs to the mancala family — games of "sowing and capturing" found in hundreds of forms across Africa, the Middle East, and Asia — and its heartland is among the Akan peoples of Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire, where it's also known as Awale or Ayo. Just how old the mancala family is remains genuinely debated: popular accounts often claim it's thousands of years old, sometimes tracing it to ancient Egypt, but the earliest archaeological evidence that scholars consider solid is a good deal more recent — stone boards carved at Aksumite sites in what's now Eritrea and Ethiopia, dated to around the 6th or 7th century CE, roughly 1,400 years ago. Boards have since been found carved into stone right across West Africa, and the game travelled with the Atlantic diaspora to the Caribbean, where it's still played today under names like Warri. Unlike some of the oldest board games, Oware's rules were never lost — they've been passed down and played continuously for centuries, refined by generations of serious players who still compete internationally, with strong players routinely calculating ten or more moves ahead.

How to Play

Oware, played much as it has been for generations across West Africa.
  1. The board. Two rows of six hollows facing each other. You own the row nearest you. Each hollow starts with 4 seeds — 48 in total.
  2. Sowing. On your turn, pick one of your own non-empty hollows, scoop up every seed in it, and drop them one at a time into each following hollow, moving counter-clockwise all the way around the board — through your own row, into your opponent's row, and back again if you have enough seeds.
  3. Capturing. If the very last seed you sow lands in an opponent's hollow and brings its count to exactly 2 or 3, you capture every seed in it. If the hollow just before that one (also on the opponent's side) also has 2 or 3 seeds, you capture that too — and so on backwards, for as long as the chain holds.
  4. You can't capture your own seeds. Only hollows on your opponent's side can ever be captured.
  5. Feeding the opponent. If your opponent's entire row is empty, you must make a move that puts at least one seed into it, if any such move is available. If none is available, the game ends and your opponent's remaining row (there being none to feed) is instead awarded to you.
  6. Winning. The first player to capture 25 or more of the 48 seeds wins outright. If the board empties out with 24 seeds captured on each side, the game is a draw.

Tips

Oware rewards counting ahead more than raw aggression — every move is fully visible to both players, so strength here comes from tracking exactly where seeds will land, not luck.
  1. Watch the 1s and 2s. A pit sitting at 1 or 2 seeds is exactly one seed from being captured. Keep as few of your own pits there as you can, and look for the same weakness in your opponent's row.
  2. Build before you release. A pit left to grow, especially towards the back of your row, gives you a longer, more flexible sow later — and a well-timed release can chain through several of your opponent's exposed pits in a single move.
  3. Be careful what you feed across. Every seed that lands in your opponent's row is a seed they might capture straight back next turn. Favour moves that keep seeds circulating on your own side while you can.
  4. Play your opening moves with intent. If you're moving first, use the early tempo to develop your row rather than rushing a capture that leaves your own pits exposed. If you're moving second, play a touch more cautiously — see what your opponent's opening exposes and look to equalise rather than force something too soon.
  5. Starvation can decide a game outright. If your opponent's row runs empty and they have no legal move left, you take everything remaining on your own side. Keep an eye on whether you can outpace their ability to refill it.
