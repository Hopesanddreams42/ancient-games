A Brief History

Hnefatafl — literally "king's table" — is a Norse strategy game that spread across Scandinavia and the Viking-settled British Isles from roughly the 5th century onward, and appears in Icelandic sagas as a favourite pastime of gods and kings alike. Archaeologists have found boards and pieces from Ireland to Norway to Sweden, but no complete rulebook survived the game's decline after chess arrived in Europe around the 11th–12th centuries. The version played here — "Copenhagen Hnefatafl" — is a modern scholarly reconstruction, pieced together from those archaeological finds and from related surviving games, such as the Sámi game tablut, whose rules were recorded by the naturalist Carl Linnaeus in 1732.

How to Play Hnefatafl

A short, step-by-step guide to the Viking "king's table" game.
  1. Meet the pieces. The King (gold) begins on the central throne, surrounded by his shield wall of defenders (bone-white). The attackers (dark iron) ring the four edges of the board.
  2. The goal. Attackers win by capturing the king. Defenders win by walking the king to any one of the four corner squares.
  3. How pieces move. Every piece — including the king — moves like a rook in chess: any number of empty squares in a straight line, horizontally or vertically. There are no diagonal moves and no jumping over other pieces.
  4. Special squares. The throne (center) and the four corners belong to the king alone. Every other piece is blocked from landing on them or passing through them.
  5. Capturing a piece. Trap an enemy piece between two of your own pieces (or one of your pieces and a hostile empty throne or corner) in a straight line, and it's removed from the board. You must move into position to spring the trap — walking a piece into a sandwich yourself is always safe.
  6. Capturing the king. The king is far tougher to catch: he's only captured when completely surrounded on all four sides by attackers (or by attackers plus the empty throne).
  7. Turn order. Attackers always move first, then the two sides alternate one move at a time.
  8. Other ways to win. If a side has no legal moves left on its turn, that side loses. Stuck on a move? The "Show a hint" button in the side panel will suggest one.

Tips

Hnefatafl is unbalanced by design — attackers usually outnumber defenders roughly two to one, so each side needs a different plan: attackers work to seal off every route to a corner, while defenders look for the one gap they need.
  1. Corners and the empty throne are hostile ground. A piece can be captured against a corner or an empty throne, not just between two of your own pieces. Use that instead of committing two pieces to every capture.
  2. Moving into a sandwich is always safe. Captures only happen to the piece that just got trapped, never to the one that moved there. Use this to reposition boldly right next to the enemy.
  3. Attackers: build a wall, don't chase the king. Spreading out to close off the ranks and files leading to all four corners does more than lunging at the king directly — a single unguarded lane is all the defenders need.
  4. Defenders: threaten two escapes at once. If you can open a clear run to two different corners in the same move, the attacker can only block one of them. A lone threat is easy to shut down; a fork usually isn't.
  5. The king fights too. Unlike a regular defender piece, the king can't be captured by an ordinary sandwich — only by attackers (or a hostile empty throne) surrounding him on all four sides at once. That makes him a surprisingly safe piece to use for springing captures of your own.
